## Part B - Survey Evaluation:

Identify and describe survey features:

1. Sample type
A sample survey with a cross-sectional design. The sample is based on a stratified design employing probability sampling. The stratification is done at the province/census metropolitan area (CMA) level, resulting 27 strata overall. An approach called 'rejective sampling' is also employed as part of the sample design. After a respondent is classified as/not a volunteer, sub-sampling is carried out for selected non-volunteer respondents. All respondents who are volunteers do a long interview. Non-volunteers are randomly divided into two groups, with one group doing a long interview, the second a short interview.

2. Sample size
A sample size of approximatively 50,000 units was used, including about 40,000 invitation letters to the electronic questionnaire sent to selected households across Canada. A completion of 24,000 questionnaires (~48%)was expected.

3. Target population
The target population is all persons 15 years of age and older living in the ten provinces of Canada, excluding full-time (residing for more than six months) residents of institutions.

4. Sampling frame
The preliminary sampling units are the groups of telephone numbers, while the final stage units are individuals within the identified households. The frame combines landline and cellular telephone numbers from the Census and various administrative sources with Statistics Canada's dwelling frame. Records are groups of one or several telephone numbers associated with the same address (or single telephone number in the case a link between a telephone number and an address could not be established). 

5. Survey mode(s)
By telephone, using combined landline and cellular telephone numbers. Information is collected from one randomly selected household member aged 15 or older, and proxy responses are not permitted.

Additional methods:
Data are collected directly from survey respondents either through an electronic questionnaire or through CATI (computer assisted telephone interviewing).
Information collected is linked to the personal tax records (T1, T1FF or T4) of respondents, and tax records of all household members.

6. Timeline
Reference period: Past 12 months preceding interview date (i.e. 2018-09-04 to 2018-12-28)
Initiation: 1985
Collection period: Every 5 years, from September to December

7. Response rate
As noted above, the expected response rate was 48% or 24,000. The actual response rate was 41.9% or 20,950.

8. Weights
The sampling weight can be interpreted as the number of population units that a given unit in the sample represents. The total population (N) is not given on the website, and the intial sample is given as 50,000 but the expected response rate is 24,000, which forms the sample (n). Statcan reports the Canadian population age 15 and over on July 1, 2018 as 31.075 million (https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1710000501&pickMembers%5B0%5D=1.1&pickMembers%5B1%5D=2.1&cubeTimeFrame.startYear=2017&cubeTimeFrame.endYear=2019&referencePeriods=20170101%2C20190101). Using this value as N, the weight of the sample is 1/(20,950/31,075,000) = 1483.

Additionally, in order to adjust for the 'rejecting' of a proportion of respondents who are not volunteers, the suvery notes tha person weight for respondents who are not 'rejected' and are not volunteers is multiplied by a factor. Finally, the weights were adjusted so that the weighted income distribution of GVP matched the 2017 CIS distribution by province.

9. Data processing
Processing used SSPE generalized processing steps to specify and run the processing of the survey. Edits were performed automatically and manually at various stages of processing at macro and micro levels, including family, consistency and flow edits.

Validation measures included:
a. Analysis of changes over time;
b. Verification of estimates through cross-tabulations;
c. Confrontation with other similar sources of data.

10. Cleaning, imputation, etc
Teh website describes the imputation process in some detail that is difficult to paraphrase, so I quote it here directly:

"Except in a few cases, all imputations were made using donor records selected through a score function. Certain characteristics on each record with item or partial non response (also called a recipient record) were compared with the characteristics on all donor records. When a characteristic was the same on the donor record and the recipient record, the donor's score increased. The donor record with the highest score was deemed the "nearest" donor and was chosen to fill in the missing information of the non respondent. If more than one donor record had the highest score, one record was randomly selected. The pool of donor records was made up in such a way that the imputed value assigned to the recipient, in conjunction with other non imputed items from the recipient, would still pass the edits. Where donor imputation could not be used, mean imputation among a pool of donors was used.

Imputation was carried out in nine steps. The first step consisted of imputing personal income and family income. The next three steps involved imputing the formal volunteering variables in the master file. Steps five and six were imputing the informal volunteering variables in the master file. Finally, the last three steps involved imputing variables in the donation file and the solicitation methods in the master file."

11. Sources of error
As noted on the website, the survey is subject to both sampling and non-sampling error.

Sampling error. Estimates based on a sample will vary from sample to sample, and typically differ from results obtained from a complete census. To create more precise estimates of the sampling variability, the bootstrap method was used to estimate the sampling variability for all of the estimates produced based on the data from 2018 GSS. The publication indicastes those estimates with high sampling variability, and all of the highlighted differences between subgroups of the population are significant at the 95% level.

Non-sampling error. Coverage errors result from households without telephones, and households with telephone services not covered by the current sampling frame, and thus excluded from the surveyed population. The GSS argues that these exclusions are small, so the expected introduced biases would also be small. Some non-response occurred at the household level, and at the individual level. Creating a frame using several linked sources improved coverage compared to random digit dialing strategies. Survey estimates were adjusted (i.e. weighted) to account for non-response cases. Other types of non-sampling errors may include response errors and processing errors. One item not mentioned is that the survey was conducted in English or French, which certain persons not fluent in either of Canada's official languages may have been more likely to be excluded or more likely to be misrepresented by a household member responding on their behalf.

12. Limitations, known biases, etc.

The main method used to reduce nonresponse bias involved a series of adjustments to the survey weights to account for nonresponse as much as possible. Additionally, basic characteristics of non-responding households, such as income and household composition, were extracted from administrative sources and then used to model and adjust nonresponse. Significant effort was made to minimize bias by using a well-tested questionnaire, a proven methodology, specialized interviewers and strict quality control. 

As a general comment, it would be useful to know more about the respondents to the GSS. Since one individual (15+) responds for all members of the household, I suspect that this individual is more likley to be a middle-aged adult, in which there are children (under 18) and/or parents (over 65) also in the household. Does this introduce a bias? How well do adults know the volunteer activities of their teenage children or their retired parent? Tough to say, but it would be valuable to conduct an analysis on the respondents (by age, sex, etc.) to see if this induces any bias.
